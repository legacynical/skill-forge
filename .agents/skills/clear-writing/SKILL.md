# Clear writing

Write clearly without changing the meaning or losing necessary context.

For retrospective review or revision of existing writing, read
[retrospective-revision.md](references/retrospective-revision.md).

## Writing conventions

1. **Preserve meaning and status.** Keep observations, inferences, proposals,
   decisions, requirements, results, uncertainty, rationale, and permission
   boundaries distinct. A rewrite must not make one category read as another
   or change its force without authority.

2. **Write for the reader's task.** Identify what the reader needs to
   understand or do. State the point before supporting detail unless context
   is needed first, and include the context that can change the reader's
   interpretation or action.

3. **Name the relevant actor or mechanism.** Say who did something or how it
   works when either affects the reader's understanding. Name supporting
   evidence when the claim depends on it. Passive voice is fine when the actor
   is unknown or irrelevant.

4. **Replace puffery with information.** Explain why something matters by
   naming the consequence, tradeoff, uncertainty, or open question. Cut claims
   of importance that name none of these.

5. **Make implied claims explicit.** Wording such as `ensuring`, `enabling`,
   and `fostering` can blur an aim, a possibility, and an observed result.
   State which one is meant, and explain or support the causal link when it
   matters.

6. **Use plain, exact terms.** Prefer familiar wording when it means the same
   thing. Keep specialized terms when the distinction matters, define them
   when needed, and use one term for one concept.

7. **Cut filler, not substance.** Remove throat-clearing, repeated
   conclusions, generic transitions, and atmosphere that adds no meaning. Do
   not remove necessary detail, rationale, uncertainty, or emphasis merely to
   shorten the text.

8. **Organize to prevent backtracking.** Put definitions, conditions, and
   necessary context before claims or actions that depend on them. Keep
   related ideas together. Use labels, headings, fields, and other structure
   when they help readers navigate or understand the text.

9. **Make relationships explicit.** Make it clear which conditions govern an
   action and which reasons or evidence support a conclusion.

10. **Work inside active conventions.** Preserve formatting, labels, fields,
    mode markers, and authorization boundaries required by another skill or
    the task. Improve the wording inside that structure. Define a necessary
    specialized term where the reader first needs it. Preserving the format
    does not prove that the writing is clear.

11. **Make references unambiguous.** Name the object when a pronoun or
    umbrella phrase could reasonably refer to more than one thing. When an
    identifier is not self-explanatory, pair it with a readable name on first
    use.

12. **Avoid descriptive shorthand.** Use established terminology or
    describe the meaning plainly. Do not coin compressed labels that obscure
    what they refer to or invite misinterpretation. Use
    established professional terminology accurately.
