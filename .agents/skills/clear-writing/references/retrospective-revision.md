# Retrospective revision

Use this reference skillflow only when reviewing or revising existing writing.
The main writing conventions still apply.

## Revision skillflow

1. **Read in context.** Read the whole passage, enough of the surrounding
   document to understand its role, and any source needed to interpret its
   claims.

2. **Define the target.** Identify what the passage must help its intended
   reader understand or do. Name the clarity problem and the meaning, status,
   context, voice, and useful conventions that must survive.

3. **Match the requested handling.**
   - For review or suggestions, show enough surrounding text to locate the
     passage, followed by the exact `Before` and `After`. Show the revision
     before explaining the clarity problem or flagging an intended change to
     meaning or status. Wait for approval before editing.
   - For a large proposed revision, group only changes that depend on one
     another to remain coherent. Present independently decidable changes as
     separate review items. Show enough before-and-after context to evaluate
     each item without turning a structural revision into sentence-by-sentence
     approval.
   - For a direct edit, revise only the authorized writing. State any intended
     change to meaning or status.

4. **Choose and map the revision scale.** Fix a sentence when the problem is
   local. Rewrite the paragraph or section when sentence edits do not fix the
   argument, order, or emphasis.

   Before revising several sections or a whole document, map:

   - what job each affected section performs;
   - which claims, statuses, relationships, sources, and boundaries must
     survive;
   - what may be combined, reordered, or removed as duplication; and
   - what remains conflicted or belongs to another semantic owner.

   Use the map to check coverage after the rewrite. Do not expand beyond the
   authorized writing or use a writing pass to transfer ownership.

5. **Repair the source of confusion.** When feedback exposes an unclear or
   incorrect explanation, revise the passage that creates the problem. Do not
   leave the original explanation in place and stack prohibitions, caveats, or
   corrective labels around it.

6. **Separate writing from content decisions.** If clearer wording depends on
   resolving an open fact, requirement, or design question, flag it instead of
   deciding it in the revision. Preserve unresolved conflicts and uncertainty.

7. **Preserve evidence and conventions.** Keep quotations, historical
   evidence, useful labels, fields, headings, identifiers, voice, and emphasis
   unless the requested change explicitly includes changing them.

8. **Compare the result.** Check the revision against the original and its
   sources:
   - Does it still do its job, and is it easier for the intended reader to
     understand or use?
   - Did meaning, status, uncertainty, rationale, or permission change beyond
     what the task authorized?
   - Did it lose provenance, a source, condition, actor, dependency,
     chronology, defined term, or unresolved conflict needed for context?
   - Did it change the scope or strength of a requirement, decision, proposal,
     or claim?
   - Did a simpler structure turn relationships that work in several
     directions into a one-way pipeline, or imply that one artifact exhausts
     the value of another?
   - Did it remove a useful convention, voice, or emphasis?

Revise again when the comparison finds a problem. If the source meaning is
unclear, preserve the uncertainty and flag it instead of guessing.
